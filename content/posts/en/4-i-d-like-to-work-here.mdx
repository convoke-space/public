Reading in Mass Niche Survival about employees being repaid for crisis-era salary cuts made me want to work there.

Earlier in the book, the company is struggling to survive. Investors are pressing for layoffs. Charlie tells employees that he won’t accept funding that comes with a requirement to let people go.

The company’s survival and its employees’ livelihoods are both at stake. I can begin to appreciate how difficult that decision must have been.

Later, after the company recovers and starts growing again, it returns the money employees had given up through salary cuts during the pandemic. That was the decision that stayed with me most.

Companies sometimes ask employees to make sacrifices when things get difficult. Remembering those sacrifices once business improves, keeping the promises made at the time, and sharing the recovery with the people who stayed are things I don’t take for granted.

There were other possible uses for that money. It could have gone toward further investment or remained in reserve. The company chose to repay its employees. Their sacrifices weren’t simply treated as something that belonged to the past.

A concept from organizational research helped me put a name to what I found so compelling. In his [2002 paper](https://doi.org/10.1287/orsc.13.1.18.543), Tony Simons describes *behavioral integrity*: how closely people perceive a manager’s actions to match their words. It pays particular attention to whether promises are kept and whether the values a manager expresses are reflected in what they do.

That is how I read the repayment in this story. The promise made during a difficult period still mattered after the immediate crisis had passed. The people who had accepted less pay were remembered when the company was in a position to return it.

Securing investment and reaching profitability are substantial achievements. But to me, keeping that promise was the company’s most memorable success.

By the time I finished the book, I was quietly rooting for Afinit and the people leading it. I don’t know them personally, and my support won’t make much difference to their business. Still, I found myself wondering what they would build next and which markets they would enter.

It’s a curious thing to become a fan of a company and its people through a book. Their decisions were described closely enough for me to care about what happened to them. Some choices worked, some were mistakes, and luck played a part, too. I appreciated how honestly that uncertainty came through.

I started reading a story about a startup finding an opportunity in India. By the end, I was thinking about the choices I would want to make when things got difficult.
