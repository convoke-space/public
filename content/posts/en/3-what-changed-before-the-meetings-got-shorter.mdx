I read the sections on meetings and workplace culture in *Mass Niche Survival* closely, because my own work involves thinking about how an organization operates.

There was a familiar face in the book, too. I’d met Haenam Chang a few times when he consulted for a startup where I used to work. This book gave me a much fuller understanding of his thinking. It also left me looking forward to seeing what flex does next.

The changes to meetings and decision-making after Steven joined were especially interesting. There was a lot I could imagine bringing into my own work.

At the company described in the book, scheduling a meeting usually meant setting aside an hour. Steven reconsidered how much time each discussion needed. Depending on the agenda, he scheduled 15, 30, or 45 minutes. Before that, he asked whether a meeting was necessary at all. If its purpose couldn’t be explained, it shouldn’t be called.

At this point, it’s tempting to open the calendar and change the default meeting length. But the pages that follow describe several other changes that made those shorter meetings possible.

Information was shared in documents beforehand, and participants were expected to arrive having read them. Meetings were for discussing points where opinions differed. If the documents and advance discussion resolved the issue, the meeting could disappear altogether. Attendance was limited to the people who needed to be there.

Once in the meeting, everyone was expected to contribute. Waiting for the boss to speak and then nodding along was treated as a problem. So was staying quiet because your view differed from everyone else’s. Important decisions had a time limit. Once a decision was made, everyone shared responsibility for carrying it out. Holding the same opinion and supporting a decision were treated as different things.

What would happen if another organization borrowed only the shorter time slots? Without enough background, participants might spend the entire meeting catching up. Without someone authorized to decide, a short meeting might simply lead to another meeting. And where people find it difficult to speak up, the absence of objections could be mistaken for sufficient discussion.

These are possibilities to consider when applying the book’s methods elsewhere. What people can accomplish in the same fifteen minutes depends on their preparation, their authority, and how they express their views.

The collaboration between the Korean and Indian teams connects with this, too. In a [published account of a book event](https://blog.afinit.com/대표님이-직접-쓰신-줄-알았어요-최인아책방에서-만난-매스니치-서바이벌-387713), Charlie describes Indian employees becoming team leaders, with Korean colleagues working as members of their teams. Changes in how people worked together came with changes in their roles and authority.

Meeting lengths, document formats, and team structures are visible. That makes them relatively easy to copy. But who contributes an opinion, who makes the decision, and how that decision gets carried out still need to be worked through. Reading these passages made me want to look more closely at those details when learning about another organization’s culture.

If I were to try the book’s approach in my own work, I’d start by checking what had been decided when a meeting ended, and who was responsible for the next action. There’s quite a bit to work through between shortening the time on the calendar and changing what happens after everyone leaves.
