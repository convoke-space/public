I’d given my AI writing assistant a second job maintaining the system, and kept getting pulled into that job myself.

I recently burned through five vouchers that reset my AI usage allowance, all in the space of ten days.

The project is something I call Content OS. It starts with a note in ChatGPT and takes an idea through drafting, review, editing, and publication. My original plan was to have Astra and Opus pass work back and forth, producing plenty of content along the way.

I was still planning to read and edit the finished drafts myself, though. I hadn’t thought enough about what that meant for the design. However quickly the AIs could produce drafts, I would still need to work through them. If reading, responding, and revising were awkward, faster production could simply leave me with more pieces waiting for attention.

Meanwhile, I’d also made ChatGPT responsible for developing and maintaining Content OS. The place where I went to work on my writing could open pull requests and request code reviews. When something got in the way of an article, the conversation could move straight into fixing the software. Tighter security controls around how data moved through the system made submitting content and moving it along more cumbersome, too.

A conversation about finishing one article now had room for improving the entire system. When ChatGPT found a problem, fixing it was both possible and part of the job I’d given it. My usage allowance went fast.

The security principle of least privilege offers a useful way to look at this. NIST describes it as limiting a user or process to the access needed for its assigned tasks. In my case, I should have worked out the assignment before deciding on the access. Helping me develop a piece of writing and keeping the whole Content OS running had ended up in the same job description.

That also meant being clearer about my own part in the work. In its guide to building agents, Anthropic describes a workflow where one model produces a response and another evaluates it, feeding suggestions back into the next attempt. It identifies clear evaluation criteria and measurable gains from repeated revision as reasons to use that approach. I can see how that could be useful for writing. But I still needed to decide where I would read the draft, contribute my thinking, and change the wording.

Looking back, I’d worked out a broad set of responsibilities for the AI, from producing content to repairing the system. I’d given much less thought to how I would actually write inside it. As the person building Content OS, I found it natural to fix whatever was blocking progress. As the person trying to use it, I kept being pulled between an article and the software behind it.

I think I should have separated those two uses from the beginning: a project for writing, and a project for development and maintenance. Finding a problem during a writing session shouldn’t automatically commit that session to fixing it. I needed a way to decide whether the repair belonged there, so that finishing one article had some reasonable boundaries.

Eventually, I reworked the process. Now I develop and finish a piece through a conversation in ChatGPT. Once I’ve done my final edit, a scheduled Claude task handles the last review and publication. Being able to talk through the writing as I go has made the whole thing much easier to use.

The original setup might have suited me if I’d been happy to publish without reading the finished pieces. But I’d planned to read and edit them myself, and I should have made that easier from the start.

These days, I work back and forth with ChatGPT on one piece at a time. Having actually tried it this way, I find it a much better fit.
