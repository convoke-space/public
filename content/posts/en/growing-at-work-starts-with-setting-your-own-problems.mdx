Reading *B밀의숲*, I found myself thinking that doing a job well includes deciding what problem to solve in the first place.

What stayed with me was the idea that uncertainty separates school from working life. In a school exam, at least the questions are given. Whether you can answer them is another matter, but you know what you are being asked to find. Someone grades your answers, and when you get something wrong, you can look up the explanation.

At work, you can get stuck before reaching that point. Something does not seem to be working, but you cannot quite say what the problem is. Does the task need to be done faster? Does the way it gets done need to change? Even after putting in considerable effort, you may still wonder whether you worked on the wrong thing.

That made me think about writing the problem down myself. What is happening now, and what would have to change for the situation to be better? It means turning a vague sense that something is wrong into a question you can work on. The question itself may be off, but putting it into words gives you something to examine.

Of course, having a question does not mean you can immediately find an answer. Some things only become clear when you try them. An approach you expected to work may fail. A wrong answer on an exam costs you points. At work, a failed attempt takes time and money, and it can affect other people. “Let’s just try it” leaves some things unresolved.

This was where the barbell strategy seemed relevant. It combines a secure base with exposure to uncertain opportunities. Applied to work, that could mean deciding how much to try while keeping the existing operation running if the new approach fails.

Suppose you come up with a way to reduce repetitive work. You could try it on a small portion of the tasks before changing the whole process. Decide how much time to spend, what you want to find out, and how to return to the previous method if something goes wrong. How far you can separate the experiment from ongoing work will vary, but you can at least consider how much you can afford to put into one attempt.

Then you need the question you wrote down at the beginning. Did the change you expected actually happen? Did making one part faster create more work somewhere else? Keeping a record of why you chose the approach, and why you changed course along the way, also lets you revisit things the final result alone cannot explain.

This is what I have in mind when I think about grading my own work. Write down how much of the problem I solved and what remains, beyond a general feeling that I did well or badly. The method may need adjusting. The original question may need rewriting. If I tried changing too much at once to tell what made a difference, I could narrow the scope next time.

That was the difference between being a student and being a working adult that resonated with me: defining the problem, working toward an answer, and reviewing the process are all part of my responsibility. Having started with uncertainty, I cannot expect my own assessment to be complete either. But I can still leave myself a record of what to change.

Apply what I learned to the next piece of work, then check whether it made a difference. Thinking about working well and growing in those terms made what I needed to do a little more concrete.
