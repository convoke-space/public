I came away from *Mass Niche Survival* a fan of Chang Byung-gyu, KRAFTON’s chairman.

Before reading it, I knew him mainly as KRAFTON’s founder and a successful entrepreneur. In the book, he’s the investor who examines reports relentlessly and keeps asking management fundamental questions about the business.

Completing every section of the reporting template was only the beginning. Could an investor understand the company’s actual position? What had changed since the previous plan? Were the risks and opportunities given a balanced account? For a company that wasn’t yet profitable, cash flow needed particularly careful attention.

I can imagine how intimidating it must have been to receive that kind of feedback after spending the night preparing a report. The next one would probably have been daunting before they had even started writing it. As a reader, though, I understood why those questions mattered. You need to know where the company stands before deciding what to do next.

Through that process, Charlie, the company’s CEO, changes how he writes to investors. When there’s a problem, he describes it as a problem. He explains his plans as plans. Even when he has an outcome he hopes to achieve, he leaves room for what he still doesn’t know.

According to the book, this greater openness builds investors’ trust. Removing the assurances that things would work out makes the company easier to understand.

That passage made me pause and think about my own work. I’ve had moments when I tried to present a situation as positively as I could. My intentions were good, but I wonder whether I sometimes made it harder for others to see what was actually happening.

The same facts can leave different impressions depending on what we lead with and what we play down. When we explain that there’s a problem but we expect to resolve it, that expectation can obscure how serious the problem currently is. The person giving the update might even start treating their own hopes as facts.

Optimism helps us keep going. It matters especially when we’re attempting something that hasn’t worked yet. But when we need to assess the present situation together, there are times when we have to set that optimism aside.

The book puts it this way: “Optimism can fuel action, but it shouldn’t fuel reporting.” I thought I already understood that principle. Reading about a company changing its reports while struggling through a crisis made it land differently.

That’s what drew me to Chang. He was willing to ask questions that could make management uncomfortable, and he kept examining the judgments behind their reports. When I come across his interviews or talks from now on, I’ll pay closer attention.

Once the problem is clearly described in a report, the next step is for the people working together to decide what to do about it.
