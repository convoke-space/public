Watching a talk by YC partner Jon Xu, I recognized my own habit of researching without settling on one thing.

There was no padding. He got straight to the point, and each point hit home. At first I thought these were principles I would want to follow if I started a business. As I listened, I began thinking about the work I am doing now.

His advice is to choose one idea and go deep. Don't try to establish in advance that it is the perfect idea. Talk to customers while building, and judge it by their response. He argues that dabbling in several ideas makes it hard to gather enough evidence to tell which ones are promising.

I spend a lot of time thinking about using AI at work, improving efficiency across a company, and finding better ways to work. I am interested in these things, and I want to do them well. But I look in so many directions that I end up unable to choose. I need to settle on something and follow through, yet time goes by while I research. The same goes for my Contents OS.

I am weak at execution. That is not difficult to admit. The problem is that after admitting it, I am still researching.

When I am looking into something, it doesn't feel much like wasting time. I have learned something. My thinking has developed. A little more research might help me make a better choice. Keeping my options open can even seem more prudent than choosing one and setting the others aside.

But there is something I miss while postponing that decision: the response I could have received by trying. Someone might have told me it was less useful than I expected. I might have found a use for it somewhere unexpected. Research can add to what I know, but the things I can learn only by doing remain unknown.

This could become an even bigger problem with something as broad as bringing AI into a company's work. Document structures, collaboration, and work processes are connected, so I want to understand the whole picture first. Looking into one thing raises questions about another. There is always another reason to keep researching.

So I want to find a starting point in a particular person's work: who is struggling with what, and what are they waiting to see change?

Suppose someone prepares a report every week by copying figures from several places. At first, automating the copying might seem sufficient. But following them through the work could reveal that most of their time goes into checking which figures are current. In that case, I would need to look first at how the data is updated and who is responsible for it. Until I looked closely enough, all of this would have fallen under the same heading: making reporting more efficient.

That is also why Jon Xu's question about understanding a customer's business well enough to run it stayed with me. Could I take over the work I am trying to change? I suspect I need to get that close to have grounds for revising the solution I started with.

I need to keep watching that person's work after I have built something, too. Has the burden decreased once entering data into the new tool, checking its output, and correcting errors are all included? I might feel proud of having finished something while the person using it has acquired one more thing to manage.

I could apply the same standard to my Contents OS. If I built it to help me publish regularly, I need to look at whether articles are actually being published. Even with things I still want to improve, I need to use it and see where I get stuck. That should make it clearer what to fix next.

I did not take choosing one thing and committing to it to mean stubbornly defending the first choice forever. I took it to mean trying it thoroughly enough to judge whether to continue or change course. That is the process I am missing right now.

I have made a note of a good talk. Next time, I want to write down what I chose and how far I actually got.
