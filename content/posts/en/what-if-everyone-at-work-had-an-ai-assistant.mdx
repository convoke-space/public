Imagine every employee having an AI assistant, and even something as simple as organizing a Google Drive folder starts to get complicated.

In my previous post, Stripe’s Orbit had me imagining colleagues joining the space where I work with Claude. When I extended that idea to the whole company, I got stuck on a question. Should I count the AI as another team member? Or think of it as a capable mini-me attached to each person—a tool, perhaps, or an assistant?

If people and AI will both use the same workspace, we may need to organize documents differently. Colleagues can often understand what “that document we discussed” refers to. The AI needs a clearer pointer. But treating it like another employee doesn’t quite fit either. Someone still has to establish what it should read, when it should start, and how far it should go.

My first thought was to give people an HTML page and the AI a Markdown document. But creating two versions also creates more things to maintain. When something changes, does it reach both? If they disagree, which one takes precedence? Something a person is still reviewing could look like an established instruction to the AI.

So I’m leaning toward having both read the same record in a form that suits them. People would see what needs their attention first. The AI would also receive the supporting information and the limits of where it applies. At the very least, they should agree on what has been decided and what is still being discussed.

I don’t think everyone needs to use AI in the same place. Someone might ask a question in Slack; someone else might need help while reading a document. Stripe describes a similar approach in its [introduction to Kai](https://stripe.dev/blog/meet-stripes-knowledge-ai-platform), where the web app, Slack, and internal tools use the same underlying APIs. What interests me more is what happens afterward. Wherever the work began, I’d like work on the same project to be findable in the same place. A colleague shouldn’t have to ask which AI I used just to find out where things stand.

Then there’s the work of getting the AI started.

After a meeting, someone has to hand over the notes, explain the situation, and ask it to extract the follow-up tasks. Once the result comes back, someone moves it into the project. AI may reduce the work in between, but the effort at either end remains. I’m not sure a company-wide approach can depend on everyone remembering to use AI at the right moment.

For example, we could have the AI extract follow-up tasks once the meeting notes have been reviewed. Where an owner or deadline is unclear, it could leave a suggestion. A person makes the decision, and that decision carries into the next step.

But “once the notes have been reviewed” needs a precise meaning. Does the AI run while someone is still writing? Does fixing a typo make it run again? What happens if a new result arrives before anyone has reviewed the previous suggestions? There are quite a few choices hidden inside the idea of making something automatic.

Calling someone’s attention to every run could also leave them with more notifications. If nothing has changed, I’d rather it just leave a record. When a decision is needed, it could bring that to the relevant person. We need to decide separately when the AI starts working and when it asks a person to step in.

And when it does ask, the decision should be easy to understand. If it proposes a schedule change, it should show why, what differs from the existing schedule, and how other work would be affected. If the person has to reread a long conversation and hunt down the documents to work this out, there is still plenty of work left for them.

I’d also like a clear distinction between an AI suggestion and a human decision. An unreviewed schedule shouldn’t turn up elsewhere as a confirmed one. The record needs to show who decided what and where that decision applies, so the next person—and their AI—can pick up from the same understanding.

But thinking this through has made me more cautious about what it means to bring the records together.

Would I feel comfortable asking questions if every conversation with AI became a shared company record? To understand a colleague’s proposal, I might deliberately look for arguments against it. Or I might assume the proposal is right, work through the implications, and drop my original doubts. If a question from the middle of that process were recorded as my position, I’d start watching my words before I even asked it.

We need a plan we can review together. Does that mean a colleague needs to read every conversation that led to it? Do thoughts I briefly considered and then abandoned belong in the project record too?

I’d like the shared record to contain what another person needs to continue the work: the basis for a proposal, the decisions made, and the questions still open. When something moves from a private conversation into the project, it should be clear who is putting it forward. People should also be able to tell what the AI drafted and what the person responsible has reviewed.

In the previous post, I imagined a colleague entering my Claude session. Now I’m thinking they may not need to see the whole conversation. I can bring out the plan we need to review and the questions we need to decide. I’d like decisions that affect our work to make it out of private conversations and into a place we can share.

Before that, I’d still like some room to change my mind. To tell the AI, “Assume I’m wrong, and let’s talk it through,” without worrying about how that might be read—and then bring my view to a colleague once I’ve had a chance to think.
