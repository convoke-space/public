Finishing my part faster with AI does not necessarily mean the work I pass to a colleague will be finished any sooner.

Anthropic’s *Working with AI* describes someone sending an IT team seven pages of instructions generated with AI. Reading that passage, I found myself thinking about the person receiving the document.

They have to work out what is being requested, check which decisions have already been made, and ask follow-up questions when requirements conflict. If the sender has not reviewed the document carefully, the recipient may end up having to organize the requirements from scratch. The effort of producing sentences has fallen, while the effort of turning those sentences into actual work remains with someone else.

Then comes the line: “Please review.” Does that mean checking the facts, agreeing on the direction, or approving the work? If the request is unclear, the reviewer first has to decide where to begin. Some of the sender’s unfinished thinking has been passed along with the document.

A similar problem has been observed in software development. A working paper analyzing companies’ work records reported that, following AI adoption, growth in code output exceeded growth in final software output. Both the time code spent waiting for review and the time spent in review increased. Although this was an observational study, it gives us reason to distinguish producing more material from completing more work.

Would preparing a better document before sending it solve the problem? It could certainly reduce some of the burden. Stating which option I recommend and why, what I have verified and what I have not, and what decision I need from the recipient makes their task much clearer.

But even a well-prepared document can get stuck waiting.

Suppose someone uses AI to automate the data gathering and analysis for a sales report. The report is ready on Monday morning. It clearly sets out what has been checked and which decisions are needed. Yet different departments use different definitions of sales, so the numbers have to be reconciled. Once that is done, a team manager reviews the report. The final decision is made at Friday’s executive meeting.

The person preparing the report has saved time. The decision it was meant to support still waits until Friday.

There is only so much that person can change alone. They can do more analysis or prepare the next report sooner. Agreeing on common definitions across departments, changing approval steps, or deciding which issues can be resolved before the regular meeting involves other people’s authority.

That is why I think organizations encouraging AI use also need to decide how much freedom employees have to change the way work gets done. Give people tools and tell them to become more efficient, and they can only act within the boundaries available to them. If work piles up at those boundaries, waiting for approval or another round of checks, individual effort has limited room to help.

When AI speeds up a task, there is an opportunity to revisit the workflow with the person receiving its output. What information do they need to make a decision? Which steps were once necessary but could now be dropped? Under what conditions could the person doing the work decide directly? Any review that remains should have a clear purpose and something specific to check.

Of course, expanding someone’s role can also become a way to give them more work. They now have AI, so they are asked to handle analysis, proposals, and customer communication, while their access to information and authority to make decisions stay the same. They become responsible for more work and have to ask permission more often. The scope of what they can bring to completion needs to expand too.

There is a related question for individuals. Is my part really finished just because I have handed an AI-generated output to a colleague? Have I prepared it well enough for them to make a decision? Am I using a request for review to pass along a decision I should have made myself? At the same time, the organization needs to examine the procedures that prevent people from finishing work even when they want to.

Looking at only one side could lead to a strange performance assessment. One person gets credit for producing more documents with AI. Another is judged slow because their work has backed up while they process those documents. A single piece of work spans both people, but their workloads are being counted separately.

When asking whether AI adoption has helped, I would want to hear from the people receiving the output as well as those producing it. Has it become easier to make a judgment? Are there fewer follow-up questions? Can decisions that used to wait actually be made sooner?
