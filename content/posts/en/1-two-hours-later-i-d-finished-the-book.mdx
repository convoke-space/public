I picked up *Mass Niche Survival* intending to skim the table of contents and ended up reading the whole book in two hours.

That doesn’t happen to me often with business books. It felt like reading a good novel. The sentences were spare, the story moved quickly, and I kept wanting to know what happened next.

The book follows Afinit, a Korean startup, as it builds TrueBalance in India. It’s a story of finding customers in an unfamiliar market, changing the business, and surviving one crisis after another.

I’ve worked at several startups, and I still find that world fascinating. I’m especially drawn to stories about products and services that solve a real problem, find customers who value them, and grow into sustainable businesses.

TrueBalance began with one such problem: checking the remaining balance on a prepaid mobile phone was a hassle. Judged by the purchasing power of an individual customer, the opportunity might have seemed small. But an enormous number of people shared that inconvenience.

The book follows the team as they seek those people out, learn how they live, and test ideas in the field. Their original plans keep changing as the business grows. Even after they think they understand their customers, they have to go back and look again.

Doing something useful for society and building a successful business don’t necessarily have to be separate ambitions. That’s something I’ve been thinking about lately. To keep providing something people need, you also have to build a business that can sustain the work. I enjoyed watching those two aims come together in this story.

I’ll admit there was something else I was looking forward to as I read.

*Surely a company that had grown this much had been through some serious trouble along the way?*

I was creating my own little teaser for what might come next. The book did not disappoint: COVID-19, dwindling runway, fundraising difficulties, an unfamiliar local business environment, even police arriving at the office. Those must have been painful times for the people involved. As a reader, I found it gripping.

Each change in the company’s circumstances puts someone’s judgment to the test. Whether to keep going, what to give up, whom to hire. We follow decisions whose outcomes the people making them couldn’t yet know. Some choices worked out. Others were mistakes. Sometimes luck helped.

The concise writing made the book a quick read. The years it covered were anything but quick for the people who lived them. What I followed over two hours had taken them years to get through.

And amid the drama, there were moments that made me think about how I work. One of them involved a report to investors.
